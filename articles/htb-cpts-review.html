01 Introduction: Why HTB CPTS Stands Out

Earning the Hack The Box Certified Penetration Testing Specialist (HTB CPTS) certification has been one of the most rewarding milestones in my offensive security journey. Unlike exams that test isolated exploit primitives or rely on artificial 24-hour timers, CPTS is engineered to validate whether you can operate as a real-world enterprise penetration tester.

There are no isolated CTF boxes with artificial hints. Instead, you are placed into the shoes of a penetration tester dropped against an interconnected corporate environment where every credential, configuration file, service ticket, and network route matters. Passing demands both technical execution across web, Linux, Windows, and Active Directory targets and the ability to deliver an exceptional commercial-grade penetration testing report.

02 18 Weeks of Prep: The 28-Module (44d 2h) Path & CherryTree Checklists

To unlock the CPTS exam voucher, you must complete 100% of the Penetration Tester Job-Role Path on HTB Academy. The path comprises 28 comprehensive modules with an official estimated learning time of 44 days and 2 hours.

However, simply reading the modules is not enough. Inclusive of completing all 28 training modules, solving every hands-on skill assessment, practicing labs, and working through IppSec's Unofficial CPTS playlist, it took me 18 weeks of consistent preparation to get exam-ready.

Note-Taking with CherryTree & Building Your Own Checklist

Throughout the 44 days and 2 hours of curriculum content, I used CherryTree as my dedicated hierarchical note-taking application. Having a fast, tree-structured notebook allowed me to organize every module by service, port, and attack phase.

More importantly: you need to build your own personal checklist. Copying someone else's cheat sheet won't help you when you're staring at an unfamiliar internal subnet on day 4 of the exam. Inside CherryTree, I maintained my own battle-tested checklists for:

  • External & Web Enumeration Checklist: Port scans, virtual host & subdomain fuzzing (ffuf), directory brute-forcing, parameter tampering, LFI/RFI, SQLi, file upload bypasses, and CMS/framework checks.
  • Pivoting & Internal Routing Checklist: Exact copy-paste commands for setting up ligolo-ng TUN interfaces, single/double/triple pivot listeners, and internal port forwards.
  • Active Directory Checklist: Unauthenticated enumeration, BloodHound collection, Kerberoasting, AS-REP Roasting, password spraying, SMB/RPC share hunting, ACL/ACE abuse (GenericAll, WriteDacl, ForceChangePassword), Kerberos Delegation, and Domain Trust abuse.
  • Post-Exploitation & Privesc Checklist: Every place to hunt for credentials on compromised Linux and Windows hosts (config files, databases, PowerShell history, scheduled tasks, registry keys, SUID/capabilities, and token privileges like SeImpersonatePrivilege).

03 IppSec's Unofficial CPTS Machines & Why AEN Is NOT the Exam

When you study a module inside HTB Academy, you inherently have a hint because you know the topic of that module. To train for a true black-box environment, I spent a significant portion of my 18-week preparation practicing on IppSec's Unofficial CPTS Prep Playlist and using ippsec.rocks.

IppSec has curated a playlist of Unofficial CPTS machines on Hack The Box that cover the exact mindset needed for the exam. Watching his walkthroughs after solving (or getting stuck on) a machine is invaluable—you learn his different enumeration techniques, how he validates assumptions rapidly, how he recovers when an exploit fails, and how he spots subtle misconfigurations that automated scanners miss.

Critical Reality Check: AEN Is NOT the Exam!
A common misconception in the community is treating the final Academy module—Attacking Enterprise Networks (AEN)—as an exact replica of the exam. AEN is NOT the exam! While AEN is great for practicing how to chain modules together, the actual CPTS exam is very different and significantly more difficult. Do not assume that cruising through AEN with hints means you can coast through the exam—expect deeper enumeration requirements, tougher rabbit holes, and much more complex multi-hop chains on the real exam.

04 Exam Pattern: Simulating a Real-World Black-Box Pentest

The HTB CPTS exam is designed to simulate a real-world commercial penetration test with you as the lead penetration tester.

When your exam begins, you receive a formal Letter of Engagement and Scope document. You are handed only an external IP address and a domain name—and your objective is to go all the way from the external perimeter deep inside the internal corporate network.

  • External-to-Internal Progression: Starting from the single external IP and domain name, you must enumerate public-facing web applications and services, breach the perimeter, discover dual-homed network interfaces, and tunnel your traffic into isolated internal subnets.
  • 10 Days Total Window: You are given 10 days to perform the assessment and submit your commercial-grade report.
  • Passing Threshold: There are 14 flags across the environment. You must capture at least 12 out of 14 flags (85 points) and submit a report that meets HTB's strict commercial grading standards.

The Lesson of Oct 27–29 & Flag 8: The Golden Rule
Going three full days (10-27, 10-28, and 10-29) without finding a single flag after capturing 7 flags in the first three days is mentally brutal. Flag 8 was by far the hardest flag on the exam. What broke the deadlock on 09-30 wasn't a lucky guess—it was following the Golden Rule: When you are stuck, go back and do enumeration again from the beginning. Re-check every compromised host, re-read every config file, re-verify yourCherryTree checklist, and look closely at output you previously skimmed over.

06 Commercial-Grade Reporting with SysReptor & Examiner's Feedback

Reaching 12 flags (85 points) is only half the battle. Your report is judged as a real commercial deliverable. To build a clean, structured, commercial-grade report without fighting Word formatting, I used SysReptor along with the reporting structure and guidelines shared in Bruno Rocha Moura's Road to CPTS.

Following SysReptor's CPTS template and Bruno Rocha Moura's reporting best practices—especially maintaining third-party auditor independence in remediation recommendations and clearly articulating business impact—paid off directly in my official evaluation.

07 Battle-Tested Tips, Checklists & The Golden Rule

Here is a summary of the key habits and strategies that carried me through 18 weeks of preparation and my Sep 24 – Oct 2 exam run:

#01 Golden Rule: Re-Enumerate When Stuck

When you hit a multi-day wall (like I did on Oct 27–29 hunting Flag 8), stop guessing exploits. Step back and do your enumeration again from scratch—the answer is almost always hiding in a service, share, or file you overlooked.

#02 Build Your Own Checklist in CherryTree

Use CherryTree during the 44d 2h Academy path to build your own structured checklists per service and attack stage. Having your own tested commands saves hours under exam pressure.

#03 Remember: AEN Is NOT the Exam

Treat Attacking Enterprise Networks (AEN) as practice, not the finish line. The real CPTS exam is very different and much harder—supplement your prep with IppSec's Unofficial CPTS machines.

#04 Use SysReptor + Bruno's Reporting Guide

Set up SysReptor before Day 1 and follow the reporting guidelines in Bruno Rocha Moura's guide. Keep remediation vendor-neutral so you maintain third-party auditor independence.

#05 Caption Every Command & Screenshot

As noted in my examiner feedback, always add clear, descriptive captions to both command output snippets and screenshots so client engineers can reproduce every step from your report alone.

#06 Track Every Credential & Pillaged File

Every time you compromise a host, pillage configuration files, scripts, and databases thoroughly and spray newly discovered credentials across all known internal subnets.
