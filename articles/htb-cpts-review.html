01 Introduction: Why HTB CPTS Stands Out

Earning the Hack The Box Certified Penetration Testing Specialist (HTB CPTS) certification has been one of the most rewarding milestones in my offensive security journey. While many industry certifications test isolated exploit primitives or place candidates under artificial 24-hour timers, CPTS is built from the ground up to evaluate whether you can operate as a full-scope enterprise penetration tester.

There are no isolated CTF boxes with random hints here. Instead, you are dropped into a cohesive, interconnected corporate environment where every credential, configuration file, service ticket, and network route matters. Passing requires both deep technical execution across web, Linux, Windows, and Active Directory targets and the ability to communicate every finding in a commercial-grade penetration testing report.

02 18 Weeks of Prep: The 28-Module (~49-Day) Penetration Tester Path

Before you can even unlock the CPTS exam voucher, Hack The Box requires 100% completion of the Penetration Tester Job-Role Path on HTB Academy. On paper, HTB Academy estimates this learning path at roughly ~49 days of continuous training across 28 densely packed modules.

In reality, if you are balancing full-time consulting work while building personal cheat sheets, taking thorough notes, and solving every skill assessment without rushing, it takes significantly longer. I dedicated around 18 weeks to complete the path and solidify my methodology.

The Golden Rule of CPTS Preparation
Everything tested on the CPTS exam is taught inside the 28 modules of the Penetration Tester Path. However, the exam will require you to chain multiple modules together across pivoted subnets without telling you which module applies. Your personal notes are your single greatest weapon.

Core Modules That Carry the Highest Weight on the Exam

  • Information Gathering — Web & Infrastructure: Subdomain and virtual host fuzzing (ffuf, gobuster), DNS zone transfers, SNMP enumeration, and service banner analysis. If you miss a vhost on day one, you can stall for hours.
  • Web Application Exploitation Modules: Covering Attacking Web Applications with Ffuf, SQLMap Essentials, Cross-Site Scripting (XSS), File Inclusion (LFI/RFI), Command Injections, File Upload Attacks, and Attacking Common Applications (Tomcat, Jenkins, Drupal, WordPress, GitLab, PRTG).
  • Pivoting, Tunneling, and Port Forwarding: Arguably the backbone of the entire exam. While the module covers SSH dynamic port forwarding, sshuttle, chisel, and socat, supplementing this module with ligolo-ng (for TUN-interface routing across double and triple pivots) makes internal enumeration exponentially smoother.
  • Active Directory Enumeration & Attacks: The heavyweight module of the path. You must be completely fluent in BloodHound / SharpHound analysis, Kerberoasting, AS-REP Roasting, Pass-the-Hash, Pass-the-Ticket, Overpass-the-Hash, ACL/ACE abuse (GenericAll, GenericWrite, WriteDacl, ForceChangePassword), GPO abuse, Kerberos delegation (Unconstrained, Constrained, and RBCD), and Domain Trust attacks (Child-to-Parent / ExtraSids and Cross-Forest trusts).
  • Linux & Windows Privilege Escalation: Service binary hijacking, SeImpersonatePrivilege (PrintSpoofer / GodPotato), UAC bypasses, scheduled tasks, SUID binaries, capabilities, cron jobs, kernel exploits, and credential hunting in configuration files and PowerShell history.
  • Documentation & Reporting: Do not skip or skim this module. HTB graders evaluate your final report against the exact standards, tone, and structure outlined here.
  • Attacking Enterprise Networks (AEN): The final capstone module is essentially a mini-CPTS exam. Treat AEN as a full dress rehearsal: solve it blind without walkthroughs, practice your multi-hop pivots, and draft a practice report in SysReptor as you go.

03 Leveling Up with IppSec & The Unofficial CPTS Track

While HTB Academy modules teach you specific vulnerabilities in a structured way, you already have a subtle hint in Academy labs because you know the title of the module you are currently studying. In a real black-box exam, nobody tells you whether a target is vulnerable to Deserialization, SQLi, or Kerberos Delegation.

To bridge that gap during my 18-week preparation, I leaned heavily on IppSec:

  1. The Unofficial CPTS Prep Playlist by IppSec: Working through IppSec's curated list of Hack The Box machines tailored for CPTS candidates trained my brain to transition from port scans to web footholds and Active Directory privesc without module prompts.
  2. Using ippsec.rocks as an Interactive Knowledge Base: Whenever I encountered a tool, protocol, or attack vector in the Academy modules that felt clunky—such as silver tickets, ADCS / ACL abuse, or pivoting edge cases—I searched the keyword on ippsec.rocks. Jumping directly to the exact timestamp in IppSec's videos to watch how he troubleshoots broken shells and enumerates live targets accelerated my learning curve tremendously.

04 Exam Pattern: Simulating a Real-World Black-Box Pentest

What makes the HTB CPTS exam special is that it does not feel like a CTF—it simulates a real-world commercial penetration test where you are the lead tester.

When you click "Start Exam", you receive a formal engagement letter and Rules of Engagement (RoE) from a fictional client. You are provided with only an external IP address and a domain name—nothing else.

[Attacker VPN] tun0 Interface
External Consultant
→
[External IP / DMZ] Web & Perimeter
Initial Foothold
→
[Internal Subnet 1] Ligolo-ng Pivot #1
AD Domain Foothold
→
[Internal Subnet 2+] Ligolo-ng Pivot #2
Cross-Domain / DC

How the Engagement Unfolds

  • Starting at the Perimeter: Armed with just that external IP and domain name, you perform black-box external reconnaissance—enumerating ports, virtual hosts, web applications, and public services to breach the DMZ.
  • Going All the Way Inside the Internal Network: Compromising the external host is just the entry ticket. Once inside, you discover dual-homed interfaces pointing to non-routable internal subnets. From there, you must establish network pivots, harvest credentials, enumerate internal Active Directory domains, move laterally across workstations and servers, and escalate privileges all the way to full domain compromise.
  • Time & Scoring Format: You get 10 full days (240 hours) for both hands-on exploitation and submitting your report. There are 14 flags distributed across the network, and you must capture at least 12 out of 14 flags and achieve a passing score on your commercial report to earn the certification.

05 My Exam Diary (Sep 24 – Oct 2): From External IP to Domain Admin

I scheduled my exam to start on September 24 and officially wrapped up and submitted my final report on October 2—completing the entire engagement and report in 9 days. Having a structured daily rhythm kept me sharp and prevented burnout.

Sep 24 (Day 1)

Engagement Kickoff & Perimeter Breach

Started the exam, reviewed the RoE scope carefully, initialized my SysReptor project and Obsidian workspace, and began enumerating the provided external IP and domain name. Discovered hidden virtual hosts and chained web vulnerabilities to gain my first foothold inside the DMZ.

Sep 25 – 26 (Days 2–3)

First Internal Pivot & Initial Active Directory Enumeration

Escalated privileges on the perimeter host, discovered internal network interfaces, and established a clean ligolo-ng tunnel into the first internal subnet. Ran internal scans, collected BloodHound data, and began mapping Active Directory users, shares, and service accounts.

Sep 27 – 29 (Days 4–6)

Deep Internal Pivoting, Lateral Movement & Domain Escalation

Set up multi-hop ligolo-ng listeners to reach deeper isolated subnets. Leveraged credential reuse, ACL misconfigurations, Kerberos delegation paths, and privilege escalation vectors across both Windows and Linux hosts—steadily progressing past the 12-flag passing threshold.

Sep 30 – Oct 2 (Days 7–9)

Verification, Evidence Audit & Final SysReptor Report Submission

Re-verified every reproduction step from scratch to ensure zero missing screenshots or command outputs. Polished the Executive Summary, CVSS v3.1 scores, attack path narrative, and remediation guidance in SysReptor, compiled the final PDF, and submitted on October 2.

06 Writing a Commercial-Grade Report with SysReptor

Many strong technical operators underestimate the reporting requirement of CPTS. Capturing 12+ flags only unlocks the door—your report is what actually determines whether you pass or fail. Hack The Box expects a commercial-grade deliverable that could be handed directly to a paying enterprise client.

Instead of wrestling with Microsoft Word formatting tables at 2:00 AM, I used SysReptor with the official HTB CPTS Report Template.

Why SysReptor Is a Game-Changer for CPTS

  • Zero Formatting Headaches: You write findings in clean Markdown (just like Obsidian), paste screenshots directly from your clipboard, and SysReptor automatically compiles a pixel-perfect PDF with a Table of Contents, severity tables, and page numbers.
  • Structured for the CPTS Rubric: The CPTS template includes dedicated sections for the Executive Summary, Scope & Methodology, Step-by-Step Attack Chain Walkthrough, and Individual Technical Findings with built-in CVSS v3.1 calculators.
  • Documenting Collateral Findings: In a real-world pentest, not every vulnerability directly hands you a flag. If you discover weak password policies, SMB signing disabled, exposed Git repositories, or excessive share permissions along the way, document them as findings in your report. Graders reward thorough, consultant-level coverage.

07 Battle-Tested Tips & Tricks to Pass CPTS

If you are currently preparing for the CPTS exam, here are the most practical lessons from my 18-week prep and my Sep 24 – Oct 2 exam run:

#01 Document in Real Time

Never say "I'll take screenshots later." Every time you exploit a vulnerability or capture a flag, immediately grab a screenshot showing whoami, hostname, ipconfig/ifconfig, and the flag value, and paste the raw command output into SysReptor.

#02 Build a Credential Matrix

Maintain a live table in your notes tracking every username, cleartext password, NTLM hash, Kerberos ticket, and SSH key you uncover—and test every new credential across all previously discovered hosts and services using netexec.

#03 Master Ligolo-ng Early

Don't wait until exam day to figure out double and triple pivots. Practice chaining ligolo-ng listeners and routes inside the Attacking Enterprise Networks (AEN) module until setting up a new subnet takes less than two minutes.

#04 Post-Exploitation Is King

Getting root or SYSTEM on a machine is not the end of that host—it is the beginning of the next one. Pillaging config files, browser data, database tables, scheduled scripts, and network connections (netstat -ano / ss -tlpn) is what unlocks the next hop.

#05 Avoid Rabbit Holes

CPTS vulnerabilities are logical and grounded in the Academy path—not obscure 0-days or guesswork. If an exploit path feels overly contrived after 2–3 hours, step back, review your enumeration output, and check if you missed a vhost, port, or BloodHound edge.

#06 Respect Your Sleep across 10 Days

Ten days is plenty of time if you pace yourself. Several of my biggest breakthroughs between Sep 24 and Oct 2 happened within 20 minutes of waking up with fresh eyes after stepping away the night before.
